1975 Māori Land March

The 1975 Māori Land March, known in te reo Māori as the Hīkoi, was a watershed moment in the modern history of Aotearoa New Zealand. It was led by the revered 79-year-old kuia Dame Whina Cooper, and this historic protest was started to directly confront and oppose the ongoing, systematic slipping away of Māori land from the Crown. The march began on 14 September 1975 from Te Hāpua in the far north of the North Island. After that, over several weeks, it would find its way across the entire length of the island, ending at the steps of Parliament in Wellington. In the end, it bought together 5,000 of supporters from different iwi, hapū and cultural heritages, in one shared stand for indigenous rights.

The movement was organized and mobilized by Te Rōpū o te Matakite ("Those Who See Ahead"), a coalition formed to protect remaining Māori land from further alienation. Their central, uncompromising rallying cry was "Not one more acre of Māori land." For generations, government policies, the Public Works Act, and unfair rating laws had stripped Māori communities of their ancestral tūrangawaewae (place to stand). By 1975, only a small fraction of New Zealand's land remained in Māori ownership. The hīkoi sought to bring global attention to these decades of Crown breaches and to demand immediate legislative protection for what little land was left.

Leaving town with just fifty people in tow, the hīkoi soon mushroomed into a nation-wide movement as it went from one town to another. Over the course of the journey, the marchers marched an extremely tough 1,000+ kilometers on State Highway 1. At night time, the marchers stayed in the nearest marae where they engaged in discussions about land rights through the process of holding lively hui or meetings. As the march continued, signatures were gathered for what became known as "Memorial of Rights," which called upon the Crown to fulfill its obligations according to the treaty and stop the sale of any more land to the Māori.
