  • Fossilisation

    The Quztacoatlus was initially discovered in 1971 by Douglas A. Lawson at the Big Bend National Park in Texas whilst conducting geological fieldwork for his masters thesis. What he found was a large hollow wing bone fragment that was later identified by Dr. Wann Langston Jr as part of a Pterosaurus wing, though massive in comparison to previous bone fragments discovered and could be more accurately compared to those of pigeons. This large bone once identified as a massive wing fragment was humanity's first introduction to a flying animal that was bigger than a car, and sent shock waves through the scientific world. Once it was realised the large pterosaur bone belonged to the species of something separate from the standard sized Pterosaur, Professor Langston dubbed the large bone fragment and new genus type as (TMM 41450-3) in 1975, otherwise known as the Quztacoatlus Northropi. ‘Quztacoatlus’(genus) as in the Aztec serpent god and ‘Jack Northropi’(species) after the American aircraft engineer for its large wingspan. Doug Lawson's discovery of the Quztacoatlus would gain massive recognition as a notable point in history and made the front page of New York Times as the ‘largest flying animal known to man’.

  • How it occurs?

    Fossilisation occurs when a living organism dies and is rapidly buried in enough sediment, this can include mud, sand, seas, lakes, rivers, or even volcanic ash. First once the organism is completely buried in sediment their soft tissue will s tart to decompose and break down, skin, flesh, internal organs and leaving anything that isn’t hardened like the bones, teeth and shells. Then over time more sediment will pile on top and the layers of sediment and bone will harden into rock with the introduction of minerals. Fossilisation like this can make an organism's skeleton last millions of years in the ground!, only being revealed to the surface once enough time and erosion have worn away at the rock encasing it and allowing it to be partially revealed for researchers to find and examine.

  • The Q.N Fossils

    In fossilisation one of the rarest types of fossilisation are from winged terrestrial insects and birds, as the soft tissue from their wings or wing membranes are incredibly difficult to preserve. In order for this type of fossilisation to occur for winged organisms with soft tissue they would have to undergo rapid sedimentation in fine-grained, oxygen-free sediment, which happens rarely. In terms of the Pterosaurs, their bones which were very thin and hollowboned for optimisation of flight and were prone to breaking down before fossilisation could even begin. This is the main reason why the rare Quztacoatlus bones are so difficult to discover as a full skeleton, and why there are no known fossilisations of their wing membranes. Leaving their overall wing size and structure as a complete mystery to researchers who could only use their gathered information on the Quztacoatlus and other Pterosaurs to theaorise if the Quztacoatlus could even fly.

  • More...

    Doug A. Lawson would also go on to study and theorise about the Quztacoatlus Northropi’s habituals, making the first suggestion that the Quztacoatlus as a scavenger feeder. This suggestion was rejected by Thomas Lehman and Langston in 1996 who theorised the Quztacoatlus fed similarly to the modern skimmers, however this was also disproven as the Skimmer and the Quztacoatlus come from different family types. It was also disproven by Mark Paul Witton and Darren Naish who claimed it would take too much energy for the Quetzalcoatlus due to excessive drag, as well as the fact most Azhdarchid remains are discovered far from large bodies of water. Later researchers would theorise the Quztacoatlus would typically feed on smaller vertebrae like storks do and swallow them whole due to lack of teeth.
