Caravaggio most famous works

'Narcissus'

Caravaggio’s painting ‘Narcissus’, created between 1597 - 1599, originates from the Greek myth of a young man, Narcissus, who was renowned for his beauty, falling in love with his own reflection in a pond. Though there are different versions of this myth, most end with his demise after realising he can’t kiss his own reflection. Leaving only a flower that bears his name known as the ‘Narcissus Flower’. It exemplifies themes of self-destruction, vanity and tragedy that are perfectly represented in Caravaggio’s painted version of the story as shown through the shadows surrounding Narcissus, the depths of the water and the detailed human expression and body language displaying grief and sadness. ‘Narcissus’ was also an example of the emerging Baroque style and was a significant subject when discussing vanity. As of today it is currently located at Galleria Nazonale d’Arte Antica in Rome and is known as one of the few works by Caravggio with mythological depictions.

'Judith Beheading Holofernes'

‘Judith Beheading Holofernes’, created between 1598 - 1599, depicts a pivotal moment scene from the Book of Judith where the Jewish widow Judith saves her city, Bethulia, by infiltrating and beheading the arrogant Assyrian general Holofernes. This story was historically viewed as a symbol for heroism, triumph of righteousness over tyranny as well as female empowerment. This particular work of Caravaggio's perfectly emulates his distinct usage of drama, danger and boldness. Highlighted by the clear use of the chiaroscuro style which uses sharp contrast between light and dark values.

'The Calling of St. Mathew'

‘The Calling of St.Matthew’ is Caravaggio’s oil painting created between 1599 - 1600, depicts the moment Jesus Christ told Mathew the tax collector to follow him and become his disciple. This painting is well known for its use in the chiaroscuro style which creates a stark shadow between light and shadow and highlights the dramatic and emotional intensity most well known for Caravaggio’s art style. This particular work was commissioned for the Contarelli Chapel in the Church of San Luigi dei Francesi, Rome. It served both as a religious devout piece and an example of Caravaggio's style, influencing countless artists, and solidifying Caravaggio’s reputation as a master of psychological depth and visual drama.
