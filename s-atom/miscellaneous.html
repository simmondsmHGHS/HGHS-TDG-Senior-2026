Miscellaneous Must-Knows

Ionisation

Ionisation is the transfer of electrons between atoms. If an atom loses electrons, it becomes positively charged, since it is losing its negative charge. If an atom gains electrons, it becomes negatively charged, as it is gaining more negative charge.

This creates positively charged ions (cations) and negatively charged ions (anions).

Isoptopes

Isotopes are versions of the same element, where they have the same number of protons, but a different number of neutrons. This affects their atomic mass, but does not affect their chemical properties as much.

An example of isotopes are carbon-14 and carbon-12, where carbon-12 has an atomic mass of 12 with 6 neutrons, and carbon-14 has an atomic mass of 14 with 8 neutrons.

Radiation

Alpha Particles

Alpha particles are hydrogen nuclei, meaning they are made up of two neutrons and two protons.

Their properties are:

  • Travel at 10% of the speed of light, so they are relatively slow.
  • Positively charged.
  • Relatively large to all other forms of radiation.
  • Highly ionising, as they lack any electrons and want to take electrons from other atoms to stabilise themselves. This means they do not travel far in the air before becoming regular hydrogen atoms.
  • Can be stopped by something as thin as a sheet of paper due to low penetration power.
  • Because of their ionising capabilities and low penetration power, they are not very dangerous from outside, but when inside a living being, as the ionisation being concentrated in small areas makes it the most dangerous type of radiation.

Beta Particles

Beta particles are small, high-energy electrons, emitted from atoms.

Their properties are:

  • Travel at 90% of the speed of light, making them the second fastest form of radiation.
  • Negatively charged.
  • Relatively small to other types of radiation.
  • Easily deflected by magnetic and electric fields due to their negligible mass. They also deflect in the opposite direction to alpha particles because of their opposite charge.
  • Moderately ionising, and can travel around a metre in the air.
  • Moderate penetrating power, they can be stopped by a metal plate at least 5mm thick.
  • Not too dangerous inside or outside of a living being, as they spread out before ionising and do not cause mass damage to a specific area.

Gamma Rays

Gamma rays are electromagnetic waves, similar to light, but with a higher frequency and shorter wavelengths. When high energy nuclei emit energy, it is in the form of gamma rays.

Their properties are:

  • Travel at the speed of light.
  • No charge.
  • Not affected by magnetic or electric fields.
  • Lowly ionising, as they can still affect atoms around them and bump their electrons around.
  • Highly penetrating, can penetrate through several centimetres of dense substances like lead.
  • Not very hazardous when emitted from inside the body as they penetrate through the skin, but can be very dangerous when emitted into the body.
