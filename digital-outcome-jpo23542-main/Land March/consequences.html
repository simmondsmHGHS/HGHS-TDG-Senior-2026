Consequences of the March

This event became especially powerful on 13 October 1975 when more than 5,000 protesters arrived at the gates of the Parliament in Wellington. The great Dame Whina Cooper took a place by the stairs and delivered the Memorial of Rights signed by more than 60,000 New Zealanders, together with the petition to the then Prime Minister of New Zealand, Bill Rowling. The presence of so many people in one place and the atmosphere of unity that they generated became the most convincing political force.

The direct impact of the hīkoi was the dramatic change in the political and legal awareness in New Zealand. While the historical Treaty of Waitangi Act was already being considered in the country’s Parliament towards the end of 1975 to establish the Waitangi Tribunal, the huge scale of the protest made a significant influence on how the government perceived the situation. As a result, the hīkoi helped to ensure the establishment of the Tribunal as a vital legal institution. Most importantly, the energy unleashed by the hīkoi in 1975 set the stage for the legislative changes introduced in 1985 to give the Waitangi Tribunal the legal authority to look into past land confiscations starting from the time of the signing of the Treaty in 1840.

In addition to the immediate changes in legislation, however, the hīkoi served to shatter the illusion that prevailed in the general psyche that race relations in New Zealand were exemplary. The fact that issues of land theft were broadcast nightly on television brought the harsh realities of the issue home to all New Zealanders, causing a great change in public awareness. For the first time in New Zealand's history, the colonial past came to be discussed on a national scale.
