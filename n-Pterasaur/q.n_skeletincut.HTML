Though there is no complete Quztacoatlus skeleton that exists, as the majority of its body was constructed through theory and careful analysis, researchers have come very close to creating an accurate full 22 foot wingspan body of the creature. The initial bone discovery by Douglas A. Lawson included a partial wing, a humerus and a wing finger of which were incredibly sized in comparison to the typical Pterosaur. Due to the nature of these bones they were able to be identified as part of the Azhdarchidae group which was the family of often gigantic Pterosaurs. Having lived 140-66 million years ago and is also one of their later evolutions of the Pterosaurs to have gone extinct. Researchers were able to use the similar bone and structure from smaller, more recognizable Pterosaurs to help inform and scale the Quztacoatlus bones to a full theoretical skeleton. In 2002 an Azhdarchid neck vertebrae was found in the Maastrichtian age Hell Creed Formation, scientists were able to use this fossil in comparison to Pterosaurs to estimate the Quztacoatlus Northropi wingspan to be 16-18 metres. Though many researchers debated whether the Quztacoatlus Northropi was capable of flight due to a proper wing membrane having never been discovered. In 2010 Donal Henderson suggested the Quztacoatlus was a flightless animal due to large mass and weight, this was countered by the Chatham University biomechanics professor Mike Habib and paleontologist Mark Witton who made the suggestion that the Quztacoatlus maximum flight range could be from 8000 to 12000 miles. Though there is no solid evidence to support this fact, multiple Quztacoatlus Northropi fossils have been found around the world, leading scientists to theorise that at one point in time it was possible.
